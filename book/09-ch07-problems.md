# Chapter 7

## Problems That Need Resolutions

Ayanokōji does not deliver a finished philosophy to a person and wait for conversion. He lets the week produce problems, and he lets each problem demand a resolution that can be *said* — by them — as if it were a conclusion.

This is slower than a speech and much faster than hoping people randomly grow.

A **problem**, here, is not only a disaster. It is a friction in their structures: a contradiction they can feel, a cost they can no longer ignore, a fear that has found a situation, a pride that has been slightly scuffed. Problems are gold because they are already *theirs*. A borrowed problem is a lecture. A native problem is a motor.

A **resolution** is not any relief. It is a formulation that settles, for now, the tension, and that settlement leans toward his end-state. The leaning must not feel like leaning. It must feel like the only adult sentence available.

### Why problems rather than visions

Visions bounce off walls. Problems get under them, because walls are built to keep out foreign meaning, not to keep out the person’s own pain.

If a student believes that raw effort is purity, you can talk all day about strategy. They will hear laziness. If that same student loses because they were predictable, the loss is a problem inside their religion. Now a sentence about using your head can be born as a heresy they themselves need.

He is a midwife of needed heresies. That sounds kind. It is kind only when the heresy is actually good for them. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the heresy is “depend on a structure I secretly stabilize.” The cognitive pattern is identical. The moral content is not.

### The chain

One resolution becomes the floor of the next problem. That is how a mind is moved a long way without a single dramatic conversion.

Example of a chain, stated as pattern rather than as a script to run on anyone:

A person is hurt by betrayal. The native problem is pain plus a story: “I was loyal, loyalty should have protected me.”

A resolution they can reach, if conversation supplies a parallel and a contrast and then a question: “Loyalty that cannot see is how you get used.” That sentence, if they say it, is a new floor.

Next week’s problem sits on that floor: a choice between a warm fool and a cold competent. They already have a language for not being used. The choice is easier. He did not make the choice. He helped the language exist.

Then a third problem: now they are colder, and someone they care about needs warmth. If he wanted them merely cynical, he would stop. If he wanted them *effective*, he might let this new problem produce a finer sentence: detachment is a tool, not a personality. Whether he wants that fineness depends on the plan. He does not worship growth for its own sake. He worships outcomes that keep him free and the field tractable. Growth is a frequent byproduct. Byproduct is not the same as goal.

### Conclusive formulation

The conversation insisted that resolutions be **conclusively formulated**. The phrase is dry and right.

A vague vibe does not hold. “I should maybe be more careful” evaporates by Thursday. “People who stay useful to their own aim don’t pay with their whole nerves every time someone leaves” can hold, especially if it was born in their mouth after a real sting.

He listens for the sentence. Until there is a sentence, the work is not done. People often feel the shift and cannot house it. Housing is language. He is good at leaving the last brick of language for them to place. If he places it, it is his brick, and they can throw it out as foreign.

This is why he asks questions that sound slightly unfinished. Unfinished questions are molds. The other person pours.

### Inducing versus finding

Sometimes the problem is already there, and he only has to stop rescuing them from it too fast. Rescuing is a way of keeping people stupid and grateful. He rarely wants stupid, even when he wants grateful.

Sometimes he induces a small problem: a piece of information at the moment of hesitation, a social configuration that presses a node, an exam role that their self-image cannot play without cracking. Induction is the part that looks most like authorship of suffering. In the story, the school already induces constantly. He is often only choosing *which* of the school’s cruelties will be educational rather than merely crushing.

The ethical line, even inside fiction, is whether the problem would have existed anyway and he merely steers the learning, or whether he manufactures pain to harvest a sentence. He does both. The book will not pretend otherwise.

### Problems as the unit of time

Ordinary people measure a relationship in talks, dates, fights. He measures in problem-resolution cycles. A week in which no native problem appeared is a week of mapping only. A week in which a resolution was formulated is a week the field changed.

This makes him seem patient and inhuman at once. Patient: he can wait for a real problem instead of forcing a fake one. Inhuman: he may regard your crisis as a useful unit. If you are inside the crisis, you want a friend. You may get a craftsman. The craftsman might save you. You might never forgive the feeling of being saved as a project.

### What this has to do with thinking

Remember the personal problem: seeing only as far as present context.

A present context *is* often a problem without a formulation. The mind panics, loops, contracts. Agency begins when the panic is treated as a problem that can be named and resolved, not as the whole of the world. He does this to others because he does it to himself. The White Room was an endless series of problems that demanded solutions without a parent translating the meaning. He became someone for whom a problem is not an atmosphere. It is a task with a possible sentence at the end.

That is transferable without becoming him. You can learn to ask, of your own now: what is the actual problem, and what would count as a conclusion? You do not need to do that to someone else.

### When a resolution is fake

Not every sentence that sounds conclusive is a floor. Some are theater: the person says the wise thing to end the talk. He can hear theater because he produces it. The body is not in the sentence. The wall is. If he needs a real floor, he will not accept the theatrical cadence. He will leave the problem alive. Leaving a problem alive is uncomfortable for people who like to be the healer in the room. He is not trying to be liked as a healer. He is trying for a settlement that will still be there on Thursday.

Fake resolutions are common in schools: after a fight, a handshake; after a failure, a speech about trying harder. The climate loves fake cadences because they look like order. He is interested in order that holds under the next exam. So he is willing to let a room stay awkward longer than a nice person would. Awkwardness is a sign that the synthesis has not arrived. Nice people fear awkwardness. He uses it as a timer.

### His own problem-chains

He is not only doing this to others. His life is a chain: how to leave the Room; how to hide in a school; how to keep a class from collapsing in a way that exposes him; how to sample attachment; how to remain unowned when a rival knows his name. Each settlement is a floor. The unfinished problem is the why of a life after winning at hiding. He keeps postponing that problem because it does not yet have a native sting strong enough to force a sentence. Comfortable emptiness does not produce conclusions. It produces more workshop.

The next chapter adds a shape to the movement from problem to resolution: not a straight line, but a tension that includes its opposite — a dialectic executed inside a singularity, so the execution is more whole.
