# Chapter 12

## Making Do, Out of Necessity

The conversation asked a clean question about Ayanokōji: does he get deeper information by making do with what he saw, out of necessity, or did he change perspective and suddenly see multiplicity as multiplicity?

The first. Almost always the first.

He does not undergo a conversion into open, relational seeing. He does not wake up one morning as a philosopher of many bodies. He takes the marks in front of him — sparse, biased, incomplete — and he forces them to yield more, because he has to. Necessity is the tutor. Perspective-shift is the myth people tell about genius.

### What “making do” is

Making do is a poor child’s epistemology that he kept after he became rich in ability.

You do not have all the files. You have a face, a timing, a rumor, a score, a seating chart. You treat that as enough to start the cycle. You act in a small way that makes the world answer. The answer is new marks. You update. You do not wait for a complete map, because complete maps arrive after the exam is over.

This is why he looks lucky. Luck is often a small action taken on a thin sample, which produced a thicker sample, which made the next action look inevitable in hindsight.

Necessity matters. Without necessity, thin samples are ignored. People daydream. He was not allowed to daydream as a way of life. A task was there. The task did not care about his inner poetry. He learned to squeeze.

### Observation plus experiment

Pure observation is passive and, after a point, vain. You can watch a person for a year and still be wrong if you never see them under a load you understand.

He adds tiny loads: a question, a delay, a piece of news, a responsibility given to someone else, a chance to betray at low stakes. The load is not always cruel. Sometimes it is a gift that tests whether they can hold a gift. The world answers. He files the answer.

This is still making do. He is not building a laboratory from nothing. He is using the school’s existing laboratory — exams, gossip, love, rank — as cheap equipment.

### Memory as a multiplier

A thin sample becomes thick if you can hold it next to a hundred other thin samples. His memory, trained until it is unfair, lets implication work. Today’s laugh-too-hard sits beside last month’s laugh-too-hard and a different person’s laugh-too-hard, and a pattern is no longer a hunch. It is a type.

People with ordinary memory think he sees more in the moment. Often he sees the same amount and *keeps* it. Keeping is a moral-neutral superpower. It turns the past into a working virtual. Bergson said the past is not gone; it presses. Ayanokōji’s trained memory is a mechanical cousin of that idea. He can let the past press on the present on purpose.

### He does not convert to ontological multiplicity

Ontological multiplicity would mean: other people are not folds of my project. Their worlds are as real as mine, not as maps but as homes.

He can say sentences that sound like that. He can act in ways that protect someone’s home. The engine still files the act under use, test, or exception. Exceptions accumulate in a life and can become a new engine. In him they have not, not fully. That is why the answer to the question is not “both, equally.” It is: necessity-driven extraction, with rare disturbances that feel like another kind of seeing and then get absorbed or postponed.

When he seems to “change perspective,” look closely. Usually he has found that a new angle yields more from the same marks. That is tactical rotation, not conversion. A photographer changing lenses is not a new religion.

### Necessity as a teacher you should not worship

Necessity made him. It also narrowed him.

If you only learn because you have to, you may not learn what no contest requires: the useless, the beautiful, the other person’s remainder. The poetic why lives in that unrequired zone. He is undereducated there. The school does not grade it. The White Room did not grade it. So his depth is the depth of a survivor-genius, not the depth of a sage.

Readers who want to “be like him” often want the extraction skill and not the starvation. This book will not sell the skill as a life. It will say: his way of knowing is extreme making-do. Extreme making-do produces extreme competence and a thin world.

### A second picture of learning

A boy is given a problem he has never seen, with tools that are not the right tools.

The perspective-shifter sits down and asks what a problem *is*, what tools *are*, whether the frame is a lie.

The maker-do uses the wrong tool until the problem admits a shape the tool can bite, or until the tool breaks in a way that reveals a better bite. Then he knows something he could not have known by sitting.

Ayanokōji is the second boy. Occasionally he is forced toward the first, when the wrongness of the frame is the whole point — when the school’s meaning-system is the enemy. Even then he tends to unscrew the frame with the frame’s own screws.

### What you can take without becoming a tool

If your problem is that you only see the present context: making-do is actually a cousin of that prison, unless you add volition. The present gives you marks. Making-do uses them. The missing piece is choosing *which cycle* the marks belong to — the loud five minutes, or the longer motion of your one being.

He chooses the longer cycle out of trained habit. You would have to choose it on purpose, repeatedly, until it is less of a heroic effort and more of a stance.

### Necessity versus curiosity

There is a fork in learning. Necessity says: extract what you must. Curiosity says: stay with what you do not yet need.

He can be curious. The novels give him a dry interest in how people work, in what a “normal” day contains, in whether he can live it. Curiosity is how the medium yields extra. But when curiosity threatens to become a life — when it would cost anonymity or rank or the father’s distance — necessity takes the wheel again. He is a genius of the first fork. Sages are people who stayed at the second long enough to be useless and, somehow, true.

If you are trying to see past present context, curiosity is actually the kinder tutor than necessity. Necessity will teach you to extract from the now. Curiosity will teach you that the now is not the only room. He used necessity because he had no choice. You might have a choice. Do not copy his tutor if you can afford a better one.

### “Out of what he saw”

The phrase matters. He does not wait for omniscience. Sight is partial. Partial sight plus a trained prior plus a test is his epistemology. Philosophers call some version of this abduction: inference to a workable explanation, then revision. He would call it obvious. The White Room did not grade you for waiting until you were sure. It graded you for not dying of hesitation.

Hesitation, in Bergson, can be a widened zone of indetermination — the living space of choice. He can use hesitation in others as a window. In himself he keeps hesitation short, except when waiting *is* the move. Short hesitation is how a tool stays a tool. Longer hesitation is how a why might enter. He is allergic to the longer kind unless it is tactical delay.

He learns deep layers from shallow data because he refuses to treat shallow data as shallow. Everything implies. That is his faith. It is a useful faith in a contest. It is a tiring faith in a life.
